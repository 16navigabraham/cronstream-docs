# Why Cronstream

The Stagnant AMM Float problem has obvious solutions that turn out to be wrong at institutional scale. Each alternative hook design fails on a different axis. Cronstream is built around the constraints the alternatives ignore.

## The comparison

| | Cronstream | Auto-compounding hooks | Fee-extracting hooks | SSTORE-based hooks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Output to LP** | Cash yield to wallet | More LP shares | Cash yield (minus fee) | Cash yield |
| **Protocol fee** | 0% | 0% | 0.5–2% | Varies |
| **Gas per distribution** | ~100 gas (TSTORE) | ~100 gas (TSTORE) | ~100 gas (TSTORE) | ~20,000 gas (SSTORE) |
| **Vampire fork risk** | None | None | High | Varies |
| **Institutional fit** | Yes | No | No | No |
| **Keeper model** | Self-funded inline | Self-funded inline | Self-funded inline | External subsidy |

## Why auto-compounding hooks fail institutions

Auto-compounding sounds efficient: take the yield surplus, convert it into liquidity, and reinvest it back into each LP's position. This is the right answer for a retail LP who wants set-and-forget exposure and doesn't think about yield accounting.

It is the wrong answer for every institution that Cronstream targets.

A treasury operations desk at a fund holding BUIDL or USDY liquidity positions cannot book an automatically increased LP share as income. Under IFRS 9 and US GAAP, the realized yield must arrive as a discrete, identified cash transfer to a controlled wallet - not as an on-chain position size increase that requires a separate exit transaction to realize. The accounting team cannot report unrealized auto-compounded LP shares to a compliance officer as the quarter's yield income.

Institutional LP yield must hit a wallet as a cash equivalent. Cronstream pushes yield in exactly this form - `IERC20.transfer()` to each LP's registered address, with no action required from the LP.

<Callout type="info">
  The distinction is: **push yield** (Cronstream) vs **grow position** (auto-compound). For retail, auto-compound is superior. For an institutional treasury, push yield is not a preference - it is a compliance requirement.
</Callout>

## Why fee-extracting hooks fail institutions

A hook that charges a performance fee on distributed yield - say, 1% of every distribution - appears commercially rational. Development costs money, and 1% of a billion-dollar TVL pool is real revenue.

The problem is that this model is trivially forkable. Any asset issuer sophisticated enough to deploy tokenized Treasuries is sophisticated enough to read a Solidity contract. They can identify the fee line, deploy a zero-fee copy of the hook, and redirect their pool to the fork in one transaction. The original protocol retains no network effect - the institutional LP immediately migrates to the fork because they receive 100% of yield instead of 99%.

A 0% fee eliminates this attack vector. There is nothing to strip out of the codebase. The protocol cannot be improved by forking it.

Cronstream funds development and operations through ecosystem grants from the Uniswap Foundation, Arbitrum, and Base - programs that exist precisely to fund non-extractive infrastructure with demonstrated TVL impact.

## Why SSTORE-based hooks fail at scale

A yield distribution hook that tracks intra-transaction state using permanent EVM storage (`SSTORE`) pays approximately [20,000 gas for every cold storage write and 2,900 gas for every warm write](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2929). In a distribution over 50 LP positions, this becomes a meaningful per-call overhead that scales linearly with pool participation.

Cronstream uses [EIP-1153](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1153) transient storage (`TSTORE`) for all intra-transaction state - reentrancy locks, gas bookkeeping, and intermediate delta accumulation. `TSTORE` costs ~100 gas per write and is automatically zeroed at the end of each transaction with no explicit cleanup. The gas efficiency moat is roughly 200× on cold writes.

Beyond cost, SSTORE-based hooks require the hook to maintain persistent storage across transactions for intermediate accounting state. This creates a category of bugs - partial state from a failed transaction contaminating the next - that transient storage eliminates by design. If a distribution reverts, every `TSTORE` slot resets to zero automatically.

## Why pull-based claim models fail operations

Some hook designs require LPs to call a `claimYield()` function themselves to receive their yield - a pull model. This transfers the gas cost to the LP and introduces an operational dependency: the LP must monitor yield accumulation and submit a transaction to claim it.

For institutional fund operations, this creates an overhead problem. A portfolio manager holding positions across 12 different pools cannot monitor 12 separate yield claim interfaces and submit transactions on a rotating schedule. The operational burden does not scale.

Cronstream is exclusively push-based. When the distribution threshold is crossed, the keeper calls `distributeYield()` and the yield arrives in each LP's wallet with no action required. The LP's operations team does not interact with the protocol at all.

## The combined moat

No alternative hook design satisfies all four institutional requirements simultaneously:

1. **Push yield as cash** - not compounded LP shares, not a claim interface
2. **Zero rent extraction** - no performance fee, no vampire fork surface
3. **Gas-efficient settlement** - EIP-1153 transient storage, not SSTORE
4. **Permissioned pool binding** - `beforeInitialize` whitelist prevents spam pool attachment

Cronstream is designed around these four constraints as non-negotiable invariants, not features to be traded off against each other.
